ITI Mechanic Diesel CBT: Engine Cycles, Fuel System & Workshop Safety Checklist

A practical, original revision map for Mechanic Diesel trainees — engine-cycle ideas, fuel-system CBT traps, cooling/lubrication/battery distinctions, and workshop safety for AITT CBT.

26 Sep 2026 · Mechanic Diesel · ~8 min read

Mechanic Diesel theory CBT rewards clear cause–effect thinking more than memorising brand catalogues. If you can explain why diesel ignites under compression, or why air in the fuel line starves injectors, twisted options become easier. This checklist is original study advice — not a copy of any NIMI book or coaching PDF. Confirm your batch syllabus on Bharat Skills.

Unofficial guide. Always verify exam pattern and dates on DGT / NCVT MIS / Skill India Digital. We do not host copyrighted NIMI PDFs.

1. Engine / cycle flash cards (memorise these)

  • 4-stroke idea: intake → compression → power → exhaust across four piston strokes (two crank revolutions). Most automotive diesel engines trainees meet are four-stroke.
  • 2-stroke idea: power events happen more often per crank revolution — different scavenging/porting ideas. Know the contrast with 4-stroke; do not invent obscure port timings for MCQs.
  • Diesel vs petrol ignition: diesel uses compression ignition — air is compressed until hot enough that injected fuel ignites. Petrol engines typically use a spark plug (spark ignition). Mixing these two ideas is a classic CBT trap.
  • TDC / BDC: Top Dead Centre = piston at the top of its travel; Bottom Dead Centre = piston at the bottom. Stroke length relates to the distance between them.
  • Bore & stroke idea: bore ≈ cylinder diameter; stroke ≈ piston travel distance. Together they shape displacement (capacity) conceptually — know the names, not random catalogue numbers.
  • Compression ratio idea: how much the trapped air (or mixture) is squeezed before combustion. Higher compression suits diesel’s compression-ignition idea; papers test the concept, not obscure model-specific ratios.

Exam tip: if an option says “diesel engines always need spark plugs to ignite fuel,” reject it. Compression ignition is the defining idea.

2. Fuel system CBT traps

Learn the chain and the symptoms — papers love mixing “no start” causes:

  • Chain idea: tank → filter(s) → pump → injector(s). Fuel must be clean, present, and free of air before injectors can meter it correctly.
  • Air in fuel: can cause hard starting, misfire, rough running, or stall — because air compresses and disrupts solid fuel delivery. Bleeding/priming ideas matter conceptually after filter work or dry tank.
  • Clogged filter symptoms (idea): power loss under load, hesitation, or starvation-like behaviour as demand rises — dirty diesel and neglected filters are high-yield workshop causes.
  • Why clean diesel & filters matter: injectors and pumps have fine clearances; water, dirt, and sludge damage parts and spoil spray patterns. Clean supply protects the whole chain.
  • Do not invent brand specs: CBT wants purpose and sequence — tank/filter/pump/injector roles — not made-up pressures or OEM part numbers.

Trap: “a clogged air filter is the same as a clogged fuel filter” is wrong. Air path ≠ fuel path; know which system each belongs to.

3. Cooling, lubrication & battery/charging — high-yield distinctions

  • Cooling purpose: removes excess combustion heat so parts stay within safe temperature — overheating risks seizure, warping, and oil breakdown. Coolant circulation + radiator/fan ideas matter more than brand thermostat numbers.
  • Lubrication purpose: reduces friction and wear, helps carry heat and contaminants, and cushions moving parts. Oil level/quality awareness beats memorising obscure viscosity codes you never practised.
  • Cooling ≠ lubrication: coolant mainly manages temperature via the cooling circuit; oil mainly lubricates. Mixing “top up oil into the radiator” style options is a common wrong distractor pattern — know which fluid goes where.
  • Battery role: stores electrical energy for starting and for electrical loads when the charging system cannot cover demand alone.
  • Charging / alternator idea: while the engine runs, the charging system replenishes the battery and supplies electrical loads. A weak battery alone is not always “alternator failed,” and a failed charging system can leave a good battery drained after running.
  • Starter vs charging: starter briefly turns the engine to start; charging keeps electrical health during run. Mixing their jobs is a frequent MCQ mix-up.

Exam tip: if an option says “engine oil’s only job is to cool the radiator,” reject it. Oil lubricates; the cooling system is the primary heat-rejection circuit.

4. Workshop safety checklist (high-yield)

  • PPE: safety shoes, eye protection, gloves suited to the task — diesel, oil, and battery acid are not “casual clothes” jobs
  • Jack stands / axle stands under a raised vehicle — never rely on a jack alone for work underneath
  • Hot surfaces: exhaust, turbo housings, coolant, and oil stay hot after shutdown — burn risk is real
  • Fuel spills: wipe/contain promptly; no smoking or open flames near diesel/petrol vapours and rags
  • Battery acid & charging: eye/skin protection; avoid shorting terminals; follow instructor rules for boost/charge
  • Running-engine hazards: rotating fans/belts, unexpected movement, exhaust in closed bays — keep clear, ventilate, follow shop lock-out habits

5. Two exemplar MCQs (original)

Practice / exemplar questions — not from any leaked paper. Written for learning only.

Q1. Which statement correctly describes diesel ignition compared with a typical spark-ignition petrol engine?
A. Diesel always needs a spark plug to ignite the fuel–air mixture every cycle
B. Diesel relies on compression ignition — hot compressed air ignites injected fuel; petrol engines typically use spark ignition
C. Both systems are identical and only differ by fuel colour
D. Diesel engines never compress air before fuel is added
Answer: B — Compression ignition vs spark ignition is the core distinction.
Q2. A trainee finishes fuel-filter service and the engine is hard to start with rough running. The most useful first idea to check among these is:
A. Ignore the fuel system and only add coolant to the battery
B. Air may have entered the fuel line — priming/bleeding and verifying the tank → filter → pump → injector path
C. Replace the radiator cap as the only possible cause
D. Assume the starter motor always causes rough running after filter work
Answer: B — Air in fuel after filter work is a classic hard-start / rough-run idea; restore a solid fuel chain.

6. One-week Mechanic Diesel theory sprint

  • Day 1–2: Engine cycles — 4-stroke vs 2-stroke idea, diesel vs petrol ignition, TDC/BDC, bore/stroke, compression ratio idea
  • Day 3: Fuel chain — tank → filter → pump → injector; air-in-fuel & clogged-filter symptom ideas
  • Day 4: Cooling vs lubrication purpose; which fluid belongs where
  • Day 5: Battery vs charging/alternator vs starter distinctions
  • Day 6: Workshop safety (PPE, stands, hot parts, spills, battery, running engine) + Employability Skills
  • Day 7: Timed Mechanic Diesel mini-mock / trade MCQs + error-notebook repair only
