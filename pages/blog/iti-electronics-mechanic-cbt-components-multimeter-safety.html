ITI Electronics Mechanic CBT: Components, Multimeter & Lab Safety Checklist

A practical, original revision map for Electronics Mechanic trainees — R/C/L and semiconductor ideas, multimeter & soldering CBT traps, resistor colour-code logic, and lab safety for AITT CBT.

26 Sep 2026 · Electronics Mechanic · ~8 min read

Electronics Mechanic theory CBT rewards clear purpose-and-behaviour thinking more than memorising part catalogues. If you can explain why a diode conducts one way, or why you measure current in series, twisted options become easier. This checklist is original study advice — not a copy of any NIMI book or coaching PDF. Confirm your batch syllabus on Bharat Skills.

Unofficial guide. Always verify exam pattern and dates on DGT / NCVT MIS / Skill India Digital. We do not host copyrighted NIMI PDFs.

1. Passive & semiconductor flash cards (memorise these)

  • Resistor: limits current and drops voltage. Unit ohm (Ω). Power rating matters so it does not overheat under load.
  • Capacitor: stores charge; blocks steady DC and passes changing signals (AC coupling idea). Unit farad (usually µF, nF, pF in practice).
  • Inductor / coil: stores energy in a magnetic field; opposes sudden current change. Unit henry (H). Know the idea, not obscure catalogue values.
  • Diode: allows current mainly in one direction (anode → cathode when forward biased). Reverse bias ≈ blocks; LEDs emit light when forward biased and polarity-correct.
  • Transistor idea (BJT): a small base current can control a larger collector–emitter current — amplification / switching. Do not invent pinouts you never practised.
  • Transformer idea: transfers AC power between circuits via magnetic coupling; turns ratio relates primary/secondary voltages conceptually. It does not “create” DC from thin air.

Exam tip: if an option says “a capacitor always passes steady DC like a wire,” reject it. Steady DC is blocked; changing signals can couple through.

2. Multimeter & measurement CBT traps

Learn what you measure and how you connect — papers love mixing series/parallel mistakes:

  • Voltage (V): measure across (parallel with) the component or supply. Wrong: opening the circuit and inserting the meter for voltage the way you would for current.
  • Current (A): measure in series with the load — the same current must flow through the meter. Parallel current measurement can blow a fuse or damage the meter.
  • Resistance (Ω): power off; isolate or remove the component when possible. Measuring resistance on a live circuit is a classic wrong habit.
  • Continuity / diode mode: quick check for open/short paths and diode forward drop idea — still power-off for continuity habits your instructor taught.
  • Range & polarity: start from a higher range if unsure; respect DC polarity and AC vs DC range selection. Guessing the lowest range first can overload the meter.

Trap: “always measure current by putting probes across the supply like voltage” is dangerous and wrong. Current = series path.

3. Colour code, soldering & PCB — high-yield distinctions

  • Resistor colour-code idea: bands encode significant digits, multiplier, and often tolerance. Practise the standard sequence on real resistors; CBT tests the method, not random memorised megaohm values.
  • Soldering purpose: make a reliable electrical and mechanical joint with solder + flux, correct tip heat, and clean pads — cold joints and bridges cause faults.
  • Cold joint vs good joint: dull/grainy or cracked joints can be intermittent; a proper joint looks wet and smooth on the pad (learn the visual idea your lab teaches).
  • ESD idea: static discharge can damage sensitive semiconductors — use mats, wrist straps, and handling habits as instructed, especially for MOS devices.
  • PCB vs breadboard: breadboard = temporary prototype without soldering; PCB = permanent copper tracks. Mixing “always solder on a breadboard” is a distractor pattern.
  • Polarised parts: electrolytic capacitors, diodes, LEDs, and many ICs have polarity/orientation — reverse fit can destroy the part or the board.

Exam tip: if an option says “measure ohms on a powered board while probes short the supply rails,” reject it. Power off for resistance; protect the meter and the circuit.

4. Electronics lab safety checklist (high-yield)

  • Eye protection when clipping leads, desoldering splash risk, or drilling/PCB work that throws debris
  • Soldering iron stand + tip away from cables; never leave a hot iron unattended on the bench edge
  • Ventilation / fume awareness — flux fumes; do not inhale deliberately; follow lab exhaust rules
  • Mains isolation: treat live equipment as live until proved otherwise; use isolation/transformer habits your instructor requires
  • Capacitor discharge idea before handling high-voltage or large filter caps — stored charge can shock after power-off
  • ESD & tool care: wrist strap/mat when required; keep liquids off boards; report damaged leads and cracked plugs

5. Two exemplar MCQs (original)

Practice / exemplar questions — not from any leaked paper. Written for learning only.

Q1. Which statement correctly describes how a typical silicon diode behaves?
A. It conducts equally well in both directions at all times
B. It mainly allows current in the forward direction when properly biased, and blocks in reverse (idealised idea)
C. It stores charge like a battery and never needs polarity attention
D. It is only used to measure ohms on a live mains socket
Answer: B — One-way conduction (forward vs reverse) is the core diode idea.
Q2. A trainee wants to measure the current drawn by a small DC lamp from a bench supply. The safest correct connection idea is:
A. Put the ammeter in parallel across the supply terminals like a voltmeter
B. Open the circuit and insert the ammeter in series so the same current flows through meter and lamp
C. Select resistance mode while the supply is still powered and short the rails
D. Always use the lowest current range first without checking the expected load
Answer: B — Current measurement is series; parallel ammeter across a supply is a common destructive mistake.

6. One-week Electronics Mechanic theory sprint

  • Day 1–2: Passive components — R, C, L purpose; units; capacitor DC-block idea
  • Day 3: Diode, LED polarity, BJT amplify/switch idea, transformer AC coupling idea
  • Day 4: Multimeter — V parallel, A series, Ω power-off; range/polarity habits
  • Day 5: Resistor colour-code method + soldering/cold-joint/ESD/PCB vs breadboard
  • Day 6: Lab safety (iron, fumes, mains, capacitor discharge, ESD) + Employability Skills
  • Day 7: Timed Electronics Mechanic mini-mock / trade MCQs + error-notebook repair only
