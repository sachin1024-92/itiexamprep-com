ITI RAC CBT: Refrigeration Cycle, Components & Safety Checklist

A practical, original revision map for Refrigeration & Air Conditioning (RAC / Mechanic Refrigeration and Air Conditioner) trainees — vapour-compression ideas, component CBT traps, refrigerants & controls favourites, and workshop safety for AITT CBT.

26 Sep 2026 · Refrigeration & Air Conditioning · ~9 min read

RAC theory CBT rewards clear purpose thinking more than memorising brand catalogues. If you can explain why the compressor raises pressure, or why the evaporator absorbs heat, twisted options become easier. This checklist is original study advice — not a copy of any NIMI book or coaching PDF. Confirm your batch syllabus on Bharat Skills.

Unofficial guide. Always verify exam pattern and dates on DGT / NCVT MIS / Skill India Digital. We do not host copyrighted NIMI PDFs.

1. Vapour-compression cycle flash cards (memorise these)

  • Core idea: a closed loop moves heat from a cold space to a warmer place using a refrigerant that changes between liquid and vapour. Cooling happens where the refrigerant evaporates and absorbs heat — not because “cold is pumped in” as a magical fluid.
  • Four stage cue: compression → condensation → expansion (throttling) → evaporation. Papers love scrambling the order; keep the pressure story clear: high-pressure side after compressor/condenser; low-pressure side after expansion device/evaporator.
  • Pressure–temperature link (concept): for a pure refrigerant in a saturated state, pressure and temperature travel together — higher saturation pressure means higher saturation temperature. This idea helps you reason about condenser vs evaporator conditions without inventing brand tables.
  • Superheat idea (high level): vapour heated above its saturation temperature at that pressure. Useful for thinking about suction-line conditions and compressor protection ideas — know the word meaning, not obscure gauge recipes from one OEM.
  • Subcooling idea (high level): liquid cooled below its saturation temperature at that pressure. Often discussed on the liquid line after the condenser — again, concept recognition beats memorising invented degree values.
  • Heat rejection vs absorption: evaporator absorbs heat from the space/load; condenser rejects heat to outdoor air or cooling water. Mixing “condenser cools the room” is a classic distractor.

Exam tip: if an option says “the condenser’s job is to absorb heat from the refrigerated space like an evaporator,” reject it. Evaporator = absorb; condenser = reject.

2. Components CBT traps (compressor, evaporator, condenser, expansion)

Learn the job of each major part — papers love swapping roles:

  • Compressor purpose: raises refrigerant vapour pressure (and temperature) so heat can be rejected in the condenser; it also keeps the refrigerant circulating. It is not a “cold maker” by itself and not a metering device.
  • Condenser purpose: rejects heat so high-pressure vapour becomes high-pressure liquid (phase change / heat rejection side). Air-cooled vs water-cooled is about how heat leaves — both still reject heat.
  • Expansion device purpose: drops pressure (and temperature) of liquid refrigerant into the low side — capillary tube, TXV/TEV, or orifice ideas. It meters flow; it does not compress vapour.
  • Evaporator purpose: low-pressure liquid/vapour absorbs heat from the load as refrigerant evaporates — this is the useful cooling effect for the space or product.
  • Oil return habit (high level): compressor oil that travels with refrigerant must return safely; poor piping practices or wrong charge habits can starve lubrication. CBT tests awareness of the idea, not illegal DIY recipes.
  • Filter-drier / receiver ideas: moisture and debris harm valves and expansion devices; receivers store liquid on some systems. Know roles at concept level — do not invent part numbers for MCQs.

Trap: “The expansion device increases refrigerant pressure like a compressor” is wrong. Expansion = pressure drop / metering; compressor = pressure rise.

3. Refrigerants & handling safety (general concepts only)

  • Approved refrigerants only: use refrigerants and procedures allowed by your instructor, employer, and applicable rules. Never invent or mix illegal/unknown gases — CBT and shop practice both expect legal, labelled cylinders and trained handling.
  • Cylinder handling: store upright as taught, protect valves, never heat cylinders with open flame, and transport with caps/guards where required. Cylinders are pressure vessels — treat them with respect.
  • Ventilation & asphyxiation idea: many refrigerants can displace oxygen in confined spaces. Work with ventilation; never enter pits or closed rooms casually after a large release — follow shop emergency rules.
  • Leak-test & vacuum habits (high level): find leaks with approved methods; evacuate moisture/air before charge as taught. Skipping vacuum/moisture control risks acid, ice at the expansion device, and poor performance — know why, not pirate “shortcut” charges.
  • Recovery / environmental idea: venting many refrigerants to atmosphere is restricted or banned depending on the gas and rules. Recovery/recycling ideas exist so ozone-depleting and high-GWP gases are not dumped — learn the principle, follow local law and instructor procedure.
  • Skin/eye cold burn caution: liquid refrigerant can cause frostbite-like injury on contact. PPE (eye protection, gloves as taught) matters during charging and recovery tasks.

Exam tip: questions about “any gas from any cylinder is fine if the system cools” are wrong. Correct type, purity, and legal handling matter.

4. Electrical / controls MCQ favourites

  • Isolation first: before opening panels or working on wiring, isolate supply as taught and prove dead. Live work is for authorised procedures only — CBT loves this safety-first habit.
  • Thermostat idea: senses temperature and commands the system on/off or modulates toward a set point. It is a control device, not the compressor itself.
  • LP / HP cut-out idea: pressure safety controls stop the compressor on unsafe low or high pressure. Mixing “HP cut-out raises pressure further” is a distractor — cut-outs protect by stopping.
  • Overload / contactor idea: motors need overload protection; contactors/relays switch compressor and fan loads. Fuse/MCB ideas protect against excess current — same “protection vs control” logic as other trades.
  • Earthing & moisture: metal cabinets and outdoor units need sound earthing; moisture in electrical boxes risks faults. Keep covers, gaskets, and cable glands in good condition as taught.
  • Fan motor roles: condenser fan helps reject heat; evaporator blower moves air across the cooling coil. Swapping which fan serves which heat exchanger is a common wrong-option pattern.

Exam tip: if an option says “a pressure cut-out exists only to make the room colder faster,” reject it. Cut-outs are safety/limit devices.

5. Workshop safety checklist (high-yield)

  • PPE: eye protection, suitable gloves, and footwear as required — especially during brazing, charging, and recovery
  • Cylinder storage & transport: upright, secured, valve protection; no open-flame heating of cylinders
  • Ventilation: work with airflow; treat large refrigerant releases as oxygen-displacement hazards in pits/closed rooms
  • Electrical isolation: lock/tag or isolate as taught before panel work; never bypass safety cut-outs for “quick tests” without instructor authority
  • Brazing / hot work: clear flammables, use screens, fire watch habits, and proper ventilation; nitrogen purge ideas where taught for clean joints
  • Lifting outdoor units & coils: team lifts or equipment; never stand under unsupported loads; secure units on roofs/stands as per site rules

6. Three exemplar MCQs (original)

Practice / exemplar questions — not from any leaked paper. Written for learning only.

Q1. Which statement best describes the main purpose of the evaporator in a vapour-compression system?
A. It raises refrigerant pressure so heat can be rejected outdoors
B. It absorbs heat from the load as low-pressure refrigerant evaporates
C. It permanently stores compressor oil so the compressor never needs lubrication
D. It increases liquid pressure exactly like a reciprocating compressor
Answer: B — Evaporator = useful heat absorption / cooling effect on the low side.
Q2. A trainee says the expansion device’s job is the same as the compressor’s. Which correction is best?
A. Both raise pressure — the expansion device is just a smaller compressor
B. The compressor raises vapour pressure; the expansion device drops liquid pressure into the low side and meters flow
C. Neither part is in the refrigeration loop
D. The expansion device only cools the condenser fan motor windings
Answer: B — Compressor = pressure rise / circulation; expansion = pressure drop / metering.
Q3. Which pair correctly matches a control/safety idea in RAC electrical systems?
A. HP cut-out — deliberately increases head pressure without limit
B. Thermostat — senses temperature and commands the system toward a set point; LP/HP cut-outs stop the compressor on unsafe pressures
C. Earthing — optional decoration on metal cabinets
D. Condenser fan — absorbs heat from the refrigerated room like an evaporator coil
Answer: B — Thermostat controls to set point; pressure cut-outs protect by stopping on unsafe LP/HP.

7. One-week RAC theory sprint

  • Day 1–2: Cycle — four stages, high vs low side, evaporator absorb vs condenser reject, superheat/subcooling words
  • Day 3: Components — compressor, condenser, expansion device, evaporator purposes + oil-return / drier ideas
  • Day 4: Refrigerants & handling — legal cylinders, ventilation, leak-test/vacuum why, recovery principle (no illegal recipes)
  • Day 5: Electrical/controls — isolation, thermostat, LP/HP cut-outs, overload/contactor, fan roles
  • Day 6: Workshop safety (PPE, cylinders, ventilation, isolation, brazing, lifting) + Employability Skills
  • Day 7: Timed RAC mini-mock / trade MCQs + error-notebook repair only
